## Not a reflection
Over the past 2 years, the current administration has taken a hammer to theoretical physics and academia as a whole. The role of academics and universities has continually been eroded and I would hazard a guess that many Americans see universities as frivolous or views them as the vegetables one needs to eat to get a job (and even this less so day by day).

Once things had settled down, and the state of academia entered its new status quo, I had intended to write a well researched post describing the effect of the policies; however, every single time I think about writing it, the administration strikes another blow to academia. 

Thus, this post is not the reflection I wanted to write, but an article describing my frustrations and opinions.

At first I was going to write a post when I heard of the NSF funding cuts, I held off to give things some time to settle down. Unfortunately, "settle down" it never did. Grants were frozen, cancelled, or just not renewed with little to no justification. DOGE was especially bad with this: a bunch of young inexperienced engineers cancelling grants with AI with no understanding of what they were cancelling. The administration, although it always had influence, started to mandate what could be taught in the classrooms. 

There are two fields that have not been touched, and in fact, have had their funding increased: AI and quantum computing. Even here, much of the money is reserved for companies and not academia. In order to get money now, it is imperative to say your research contributes or uses one of these two fields (usually AI). Despite this, it is not like I want this funding taken away. When the superconducting supercollider (SSC) was being built in Texas toward the end of the 20th century, Anderson was arguing to congress that the funding could be better used by condensed matter physicists. What he failed to realize is that the science funding is not a zero sum game, if the SSC was cancelled, that money was going to evaporate back into congress and the condensed matter physicists were not going to get it. Indeed, this is exactly what happened when the SSC was cancelled. If we learn from our past, it is no use demanding AI and QC be defunded to give money to other fields.

There were also restrictions placed on international collaboration. Da-chuan left to visit China for a winter break and we were unable to contact him without using his personal email. His visa to the US (where he had a postdoc at Harvard) was delayed. 

It is comical that earlier this year, the entire NSF science advisory was fired. It is even more hilarious that the list of [current president's science advisors](https://www.whitehouse.gov/releases/2026/03/president-trump-announces-appointments-to-presidents-council-of-advisors-on-science-and-technology/) only has **one** scientist on it. Most everyone else is a tech executive. 

For international students things are getting progressively worse. It is harder to get into the country and even harder to stay here. A new [DHS ruling ](https://www.dhs.gov/news/2026/07/16/trump-administration-issues-final-rule-end-foreign-student-visa-abuse)caps the length of a degree at four years for international students. This *may* be extended, but who knows how difficult it will be to get an extension.

The above makes it much harder for universities to accept international students, and for international students to come to the US. Why would professors take a risk by hiring international students if they could get kicked out in 4 years? Why would an international student want to risk it? The brain drain in the US continues and is sped up by these rulings. There is a [proposition](https://www.wsj.com/politics/policy/u-s-weighs-100-000-fee-for-foreign-students-wanting-to-work-after-graduation-d0bf43d9?st=mES1e9&reflink=desktopwebshare_permalink) that would make it even harder for students to stay in the US. So now out of the students the US does train, we just send them back to their country after they're done.

The worst is yet to come, [OMB-2026-0034](https://www.regulations.gov/docket/OMB-2026-0034), if passed, would be the nail in the coffin for academia in the US. It would destroy funding, peer review, international collaboration, and more.  

If I were writing a a storybook about how science is dismantled in a fictional country following the above, I think people would write it off as unrealistic and too Draconian. 

Before World War II, the center of science was arguable Germany. A mass exodus of scientists, teachers, and other academics took that title away. This seems to be the trend in the US as well. While the US and much of Europe are cutting funding, much of Asia, India, China, Japan, etc. are *increasing* their funding.

Out of all the grad students I have known, the ones that got a post-doc are all going overseas. In my circle, pretty much everyone is planning on leaving the country to continue physics. There does not seem to be much of a future here. 

Maybe in two years the needle swings back the other way and things magically go back to the status quo. This is unlikely, people have long memories and the people that forced the current situation to happen, the politicians and voters, will still be here.

In any case, is it worth taking the risk to stay?

## Forcing research in industry
Underlying much of the administration's rulings seems to be the claim that nothing new happens in universities now, all current and future advances will happen in R&D departments in industry. With the current advisors, this is not too much of a surprise. If I tell you how stupid this is, I would be preaching to the choir. R&D departments are focused on developments that will produce a return on investment in a few short years. This is not the timescale academic research works on. Academic research provides an ROI on the scale of decades, if not centuries. 

Despite this, it seems like the above mentality of industry over research is quite common among Americans. I find this mentality not only online, but in real life when I talk to people. I found this mentality especially prevalent among my CS friends and professors in my undergrad.

Eric Weinstein visited UT a while back to give a "talk" on how to bring science funding back into academia. I knew it was going to be useless and yet I still went. What followed was four hours of casual misogyny, racism, and a complex to be recognized by the scientific community while actively trying to dismantle it. Sometimes with these types of personalities, it is difficult to know whether it is an act or if they truly believe in what they say. Weinstein definitely thinks he is much smarter than he actually is. 

Despite saying he supported academia, it is very clear he believed the above to be true and academia hadn't accomplished anything in decades. This is also what he did when going on Joe Rogan. He called people in UT who didn't want him to come the "string theory cabal". I am very proud of this label and am going to put it on my CV. I was going to go in depth on his "talk", but after thinking for a while, I realized it isn't worth it. There's a transcript <a href="/posts/physics_life/documents/transcript.pdf">

## Druck
This section is largely disconnected from the above. I recently finished Milton Mayer's *They thought they were free*, written after world war II where he interviewed the common men in the Nazi party. He wanted to understand how it got to that point. I cannot do the book justice, but a couple of pieces of the text are worth mentioning. 

Mayer repeatedly draws parallels between Germany and America to show why things happened the way they did in Germany, but not in America. He points to several cultural, social, and political manners of Americans which safeguard against what happened to the Germans during the Weimar republic and into the national socialist party takeover. Alarmingly, these safeguards that he points to, seem to have been eroded in recent years. 

Among many other things, he points to the erasure of academic freedom, a rise of anti-intellectualism, driving away the most educated.

Of particular interest to me were <a href="/posts/physics_life/documents/mayer1314.pdf">
  chapters 13 to 14
</a> where Mayer interviews a philologist. If you made it this far, you may as well read it, it's not too long. 

One main thing he talks about how slowly things devolved and how he (nor his colleagues) didn't do anything. There was a thought that they might be alarmists. That things were not too bad, and even if they were, things would course correct soon.
>"But the one great shocking occasion, when tens or hundreds or thousands will join with you, never comes. That's the difficulty. If the last and worst act of the whole regime had come immediately after the first and smallest, thousands, yes, millions would have been sufficiently shocked -if, let us say, the gassing of the Jews in '43 had come immediately after the 'German Firm' stickers on the windows of non-Jewish shops in '33. But of course this isn't the way it happens. In between come all the hundreds of little steps, some of them imperceptible, each of them preparing you not to be shocked by the next. Step C is not so much worse than Step B, and, if you did not make a stand at Step B, why should you at Step C? And so on to Step D.

The philologist and his wife, the bourgeois, not among the 10 "little men" Mayer was interviewing, pointed out how those opposed to the national socialist party gave up on trying to convince those in it, the "little men", to understand what was going on. They started trying to conserve energy, when they realized what was happening.  

In talking about the ordinary men that allowed the country to deteriorate (where Mayer claimed ordinary men were ordinary in both America and Germany), the wife claimed the difference between Americans and Germans was "*farsightedness*". The Americans were under less pressure, *Druck*, and freer to take the long view.  

These days it does not seem like we have less pressure. It does not seem like we are taking the long view. 

Often times in my close circles, we talk about what we may have done in those days. Despite how it sometimes feels, we (graduate students, academics, professors) are also the bourgeois, not "little men". 

A chemical engineer Mayer talked to had the choice to leave his profession, maybe the country, or join the Nazi party to retain his job. After weighing options, he decided if he stayed, he could at least try and save some. Indeed, he saved maybe a dozen by sheltering them during the war. He completely regrets his choice. When asked to explain:
> I will explain. There I was, in 1935, a perfect example of the kind of person who, with all his advantages in birth, in education, and in position, rules (or might easily rule) in any country. If I had refused to take the oath in 1935, it would have meant that thousands and thousands like me, all over Germany, were refusing to take it. Their refusal would have heartened millions. Thus the regime would have been overthrown, or, indeed, would never have come to power in the first place. The fact that I was not prepared to resist, in 1935, meant that all the thousands, hundreds of thousands, like me in Germany were also unprepared, and each one of these hundreds of thousands was, like me, a man of great influence or of great potential influence.  Thus the world was lost.

So now maybe step A was the erosion of civil liberties, maybe step B was the hammer against education and academia, maybe step C were the killings by ICE agents on the street of our cities or the [many dead in detention camps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_deaths_in_ICE_detention) on the border, and yet I sit here doing nothing. 

I vote, I protest, I write to my congressman about my concerns. I talk with friends and colleagues about what is happening, and yet it continues to happen. Things are not getting any better. 

And yet, what else can I do? What else is there *to* do?

I wonder what step D will be.
