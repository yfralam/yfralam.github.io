## Late night blues

A few weeks ago, I was in the office until 12 AM. I had just finished deciphering a cryptic email from Jacques[^1] and decided to call it a day. As I was walking out with another officemate, I saw one of my friends, Dio, along with his advisor, Katie Freese, having a lively discussion in his office. Seeing graduate students work late into the night or even stay overnight is not unheard of, but seeing a professor in the department at midnight made us do a double take. 

They didn't seem to leave any room for us to interject a "goodbye" (nor did I have the braincells to listen to such heated physics discussion so late), so I simply waved to Dio and left. 

I came in the following morning and saw Dio having a lively discussion in another friend's office so I walked in and asked, "Why were you and Katie here so late?" Alex Lu said Dio discovered supersymmetry. I ignored Alex.

Little did I know that the experiment, Lux Zeplin (LZ), a giant tank of xenon in South Dakota built to look for Dark Matter, found an event at around 3 sigma which could not be explained from the Standard Model or as noise. Dio was reading the paper that very day and found that a very good candidate that matched the data was the Higgsino, the supersymmetric partner to the Higgs. That's why he and Katie were at the office so late at night; they pushed out a [paper](https://inspirehep.net/literature/3198592) at 4 AM right after they saw the data. I apologized to Alex.

## Chasing the sirens

It would be very exciting if it turned out that Dark Matter was a supersymmetry particle. This would kill a non-trivial number of birds with one very heavy stone. Pinning down the mass of the Higgsino would also tell us about where we might find the other superpartners. When they wrote the paper, it seemed that the mass (if the particle is thermal) would be about 1 TeV. Heavy, but well within reach of colliders.

The issue seems to be with solar neutrinos. If this was what was going on, we would see a bunch of solar neutrinos due to the Higgsino. The fact that we don't essentially kills the thermal Higgsino proposal. 

One can still salvage it by making the Higgsino non-thermal. In this case mass becomes a free parameter and to reproduce the results of the experiment, the mass must be 10 - 100 TeV. This makes it obvious why we haven't seen it in a collider, but is still good news as that energy is not out of the question of reaching in the future. 

The other alternative is through some sort of dark photon, though I admit I'm not too familiar with this proposal.

You should take the physics above with a grain of salt. I understand most of the above from talking to Dio and other phenomenologists in the department. 

The good news is that if it *is* a non-thermal Higgsino, LZ should be able to see other events at a bit higher energy. This one event should be the first of many. Dio has told me that the experimentalists are calibrating LZ to start looking at the higher energies. If they find more, it would be a very good sign.

As physicists, we are inherently conservatives when it comes to new physics, as we should be. That being said, especially as someone who is only looking at this from the outside, it doesn't hurt to be optimistic[^2]. Maybe I'll sit down and learn MSSM finally.
